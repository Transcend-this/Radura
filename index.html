    Why a daily puzzle?

    Thinking problems such as slower processing, attention lapses and memory slips affect roughly 40–70% of people with MS, and they are common in other neurological and autoimmune conditions too.

    A 2019 meta-analysis of 20 randomized trials in MS (Lampit et al., Neurorehabilitation and Neural Repair) found that computerized cognitive training improved overall thinking performance by a modest, real amount (effect size 0.30). Gains showed up in attention and processing speed, executive function, and verbal and visual memory.

    • Each weekday rotates through those trained skills, and every puzzle is newly generated from the date.
    • Difficulty adapts to your last score on that puzzle type.
    • Gains faded when training stopped, so a little every day beats a lot once.
    • It trains thinking skills; it does not repair nerves. Evidence on fatigue and daily function was inconclusive, and studies outside MS are fewer.

    Having formal memory or speed testing (such as the SDMT)? Tell the examiner you do daily brain training.

    How it works, privacy, and tests

    The plan comes from fixed rules, not an AI: battery math (rest adds charge, activity spends it), safe-swallowing and speech-practice basics from speech-language therapy, and pacing guidance for people who crash after exertion. Morning links come from your own history: once about 10 nights are logged, the page compares what you did the evening before with how you woke up. Support scores follow the Neuro-Centric Dashboard: each part is capped, and sleep scales the result. History is saved privately to the signed-in person's own space; not even the page's owner can read it.
